The ADHD Tax: What It Is and How to Reduce It

The “ADHD tax” describes the extra money that can be lost through missed deadlines, forgotten subscriptions, duplicate purchases, food waste, replacement buying and other avoidable costs.

In this guide
  • Common ADHD tax examples
  • Run an ADHD Tax Audit
  • Reduce repeat costs with systems
  • Use a quarterly checkup

Common ADHD tax examples

The book identifies several patterns that can add up over time:

  • Late fees and missed payment deadlines
  • Impulse purchases
  • Food waste
  • Duplicate purchases
  • Forgotten subscriptions
  • Buying replacements for items you cannot find
  • Missed renewal or process fees

The purpose of naming these costs is not to create guilt. It is to make an invisible category visible so you can design around it.

Run an ADHD Tax Audit

Start with the previous month. Look for fees, duplicate purchases, forgotten subscriptions, wasted groceries, replacement purchases and purchases made because a deadline or task was forgotten.

  1. Find the transaction.
  2. Label the type of ADHD tax.
  3. Estimate the amount.
  4. Ask what system could prevent the same cost next time.
  5. Choose one prevention step.
Important: The goal is not a perfect number. The audit is a visibility tool that helps you spot patterns.

Reduce repeat costs with systems

Use automation for recurring bills, visible account structures for important balances, friction for impulse purchases and recurring check-ins for tasks that otherwise disappear from view.

If a problem repeats three times, the book's Rule of Three says to examine the system design. Ask whether a reminder, automation, checklist, buffer or different account structure could remove the need to remember.

Use a quarterly checkup

The book recommends checking your money system every 90 days. Review subscriptions, automated transfers, your tax number or other relevant financial figures, thresholds, wishlists, major money rules and your “fun” spending.
