Why Do People With ADHD Overspend? 10 Common Triggers

ADHD overspending can have more than one trigger. Understanding the pattern can help you build a pause between the trigger and the purchase.

In this guide
  • Why overspending can feel automatic
  • 10 common triggers
  • What to do when a trigger appears
  • A simple starting plan

Why overspending can feel automatic

The book describes money behavior through the lens of an interest-based nervous system. Novelty, urgency, challenge and emotional intensity can make some actions much easier to start than boring administrative tasks.

That does not mean every purchase has the same cause. It means it can be useful to look for the moment before the purchase: what was happening, what were you feeling, and what made the purchase unusually easy?

1. Novelty

A new product can promise a quick spark. The book connects novelty-seeking with impulse purchases that can later become unused clutter.

2. Boredom

Shopping can provide stimulation when the current task feels flat. A wish list and a delay rule can create a pause without demanding that you eliminate the desire completely.

3. Stress or difficult feelings

The book discusses spending as a form of emotional relief. If a purchase appears during a difficult moment, record the trigger before deciding whether to buy.

4. The feeling of becoming a new person

Sometimes the purchase is really about an imagined future self: the organized person, the fitness person, the creative person. The book suggests asking whether you are buying a thing or buying the person you hope to become.

5. Friction-free checkout

Saved cards, one-tap checkout and logged-in shopping make the distance between wanting and buying very small. Removing saved payment details adds a deliberate pause.

6. Retail marketing

Sales emails, shopping notifications and constant product recommendations can repeatedly place purchases in front of you. Unsubscribing and turning off shopping notifications reduces some triggers.

7. Fear of missing out

Urgency can turn a want into a perceived emergency. A 24-hour rule is one of the book's core friction tools for separating urgency from importance.

8. Money avoidance

When bills or balances feel uncomfortable, avoiding the money app can make the situation harder to see. The book recommends short recurring Money Dates rather than waiting until money feels urgent.

9. The "I've already messed up" spiral

One unplanned purchase can become a reason to abandon the rest of the week. The zero-judgment recovery protocol says to restart rather than treating the slip as proof that the entire system failed.

10. No defined Fun money

If every enjoyable purchase feels forbidden, the plan can become hard to sustain. The book gives Fun money a legitimate place in the system so spending for enjoyment does not have to be treated as a failure.

What to do when a trigger appears

  1. Name the trigger: bored, stressed, excited, avoiding, or responding to a sale.
  2. Move the item to a Wish List instead of checkout.
  3. Wait 24 hours for ordinary impulse purchases.
  4. Remove saved payment information from the store if needed.
  5. If the purchase is large, use a higher threshold and involve another person.
The goal is not "never want anything." The goal is to create enough space for a deliberate decision.

A simple starting plan

Pick one trigger you recognize most often and install one friction step today. Then review what happened during your next Money Date.
