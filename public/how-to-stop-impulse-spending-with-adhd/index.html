How to Stop Impulse Spending With ADHD: 10 Practical Strategies That Actually Work

Impulse spending can be especially frustrating when you have ADHD. Instead of asking willpower to make every spending decision, build a money system that creates space between “I want this” and “I bought this.”

Why impulse spending can feel so compelling

You may genuinely intend to save money, follow a budget, or avoid unnecessary purchases. Then something interesting, exciting, useful, or suddenly “on sale” appears—and you buy it before you have time to decide whether you actually need it.

Afterward can come the familiar cycle: buy, regret, feel guilty, promise to do better, repeat.

The practical alternative is to stop making willpower responsible for every purchase. Create systems that make an automatic purchase a little less automatic.

1. Use a 24-hour rule before buying

When you see something you weren't planning to buy, don't decide immediately. Put it on a Wish List instead.

Your Wish List can be a simple note on your phone containing the item, price and date you wanted it. For larger purchases, use a longer waiting period.

The goal isn't to stop yourself from buying everything you want. It is to turn an automatic purchase into a conscious decision.

Try this today: Create a phone note called “WISH LIST — WAIT BEFORE BUYING” and put your next unplanned purchase there instead of checking out.

2. Make shopping slightly harder

Modern shopping removes friction: saved cards, one-click checkout, shopping apps and instant payment. You can deliberately put some friction back.

  • Remove saved cards from your favorite shopping websites.
  • Remove payment information from delivery apps.
  • Log out of shopping accounts.
  • Turn off shopping-app notifications.
  • Unsubscribe from promotional emails.
  • Keep your physical wallet or card somewhere less convenient.

You aren't trying to make shopping impossible. You're creating a few seconds in which you can reconsider.

3. Stop treating your shopping cart like a Wish List

Your online cart can create the feeling that you've already decided to buy. Instead, move interesting purchases into a separate Wish List and empty your carts.

Review the Wish List later and ask: Do I still want this now that I'm no longer in the excitement of finding it?

Some purchases will survive the waiting period. Others won't. Both outcomes are useful.

4. Identify your personal spending triggers

Impulse spending usually has a context. Pay attention to when you tend to spend.

  • Bored?
  • Stressed?
  • Tired?
  • Lonely?
  • Celebrating?
  • Procrastinating?
  • Avoiding an unpleasant task?
  • Excited about starting something new?

When you feel an urge to buy, record the item, price, what you were feeling, and whether you bought it. After a few weeks, patterns may become easier to see.

5. Remove the triggers you don't need

You don't have to prove that you can resist every sale. Reduce the number of sales asking for your attention.

Try an “Unsubscribe Day.” Remove retail newsletters, flash-sale emails, promotional alerts and unnecessary shopping notifications. Fewer triggers can mean fewer spending decisions.

6. Give yourself a guilt-free Fun money category

A money system that says “you are never allowed to spend money on fun” can be difficult to maintain. Instead, create a defined amount of money that is genuinely yours to spend.

Call it FUN, or use a name that makes sense to you. The important part is that the money has a job.

When you spend from that category, you're not automatically taking money from bills or emergency savings. You're using the system as intended.

That changes the question from “Am I allowed to spend money?” to “Is there money available in my Fun category?”

7. Make your money visible

If your money is spread across accounts, bills, savings, cards and subscriptions, your current bank balance may not tell the whole story.

Make your money easier to see. You could create separate pots or categories for Bills, Groceries/Life, Fun, emergency savings and other predictable expenses.

A short weekly 15-minute Money Date can help you reconnect with what is happening without turning money management into an all-day project.

8. Create a bigger-purchase rule

Small purchases can add up, while large impulse purchases can create much bigger problems. Create a separate rule for expensive purchases.

Choose a threshold that makes sense for your financial situation. For example: anything over your chosen threshold requires a waiting period and another person's perspective.

  1. Put it on your Wish List.
  2. Wait.
  3. Review the actual cost.
  4. Consider how realistically you'll use it.
  5. Talk to someone you trust.
  6. Then decide.

This can be particularly useful for purchases connected to a sudden burst of motivation, such as expensive equipment, courses or tools for a new project.

9. Don't turn one bad purchase into a bad week

You make an impulse purchase. Then you think, “I've already messed up this month, so what's the point?” The original mistake can become several more purchases.

Instead, use a recovery rule: notice it, learn from it, move on.

Ask what triggered the purchase and what could have created more friction. Then make one small system change.

A broken rule is information. It doesn't have to become a verdict about you.

10. Build a system instead of depending on willpower

The biggest shift is to stop asking yourself to remember everything.

  • Automate what you can.
  • Create recurring reminders.
  • Separate money into categories.
  • Use a Wish List.
  • Add waiting periods.
  • Remove payment information.
  • Turn off shopping notifications.
  • Schedule a weekly Money Date.
  • Give yourself designated Fun money.

The goal is to make the right financial action easier to repeat—even when you're distracted, bored, tired, busy or overwhelmed.

What if you already made an impulse purchase?

Don't panic.

If you can return it

Return it. Don't let embarrassment prevent you from recovering the money.

If you can't return it

Ask what the purchase can teach you. Was it a boredom purchase, stress purchase, novelty purchase, sale-trigger purchase, or an attempt to solve a problem immediately?

Then change one thing

Remove a saved card. Create a Wish List. Turn off a notification. Establish a spending threshold. You don't need to redesign your entire financial life because of one purchase.

Frequently Asked Questions About ADHD and Impulse Spending

Why does ADHD make impulse spending difficult?

People with ADHD can experience difficulties with areas such as attention, planning, organization and delaying actions. These challenges can make money systems that depend heavily on remembering and consistent self-control harder to maintain. Experiences vary, so the practical goal is to identify your own patterns and build systems around them.

How do I stop impulse buying with ADHD?

Start by not buying unplanned purchases immediately. Put the item on a Wish List and wait. Then add friction by removing saved payment information and shopping notifications.

Does the 24-hour rule work for ADHD?

A waiting rule can be a useful friction strategy because it creates time between the urge to buy and the actual purchase. The exact waiting period should be realistic for you. Larger purchases may deserve a longer wait and another person's perspective.

How can I stop online shopping with ADHD?

Make online shopping less immediate: remove saved cards, log out, turn off notifications, unsubscribe from promotional emails, use a Wish List, empty shopping carts and create a spending threshold.

Should people with ADHD avoid spending money on fun?

Not necessarily. A defined Fun category can make discretionary spending intentional rather than something that repeatedly competes with essential expenses. The amount should reflect your actual income, expenses and priorities.

What should I do after an impulse purchase?

Don't turn one purchase into a financial collapse. Look at what happened, identify the trigger, make one small system change, and move forward.

The Bottom Line

If impulse spending has been a recurring problem, trying harder may not be the most useful solution. Change the environment around the decision.

Put purchases on a Wish List. Create a waiting period. Remove saved payment details. Turn off shopping notifications. Give yourself legitimate Fun money. Make your available money visible. Create rules for larger purchases. And when you slip, recover without turning the mistake into a shame spiral.

The objective isn't to become someone who never wants to buy anything. It's to create enough space between wanting and buying that you get to make the decision.
