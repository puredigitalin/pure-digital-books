How to Manage Money With ADHD Without Relying on a Traditional Budget

If traditional budgets keep getting started and abandoned, try designing a money system around automation, visibility and simple rules instead.

In this guide
  • Why traditional budgeting can be hard to maintain
  • Use a simple pot system
  • Automate recurring money tasks
  • Make the right number visible
  • Use rules instead of constant decisions
  • Know how to restart

Why traditional budgeting can be hard to maintain

The book argues that conventional money advice often asks for planning, remembering, resisting and repeated follow-through. Those are exactly the kinds of executive-function tasks that can become difficult when money is boring, distant or emotionally loaded.

The alternative is not to ignore money. It is to move as much of the work as possible into systems that run with fewer decisions.

1. Use a simple pot system

The book's account architecture uses three buckets: Bills, Safety Vault and Everything Else. Bills holds fixed monthly costs plus a buffer. Safety Vault holds emergency savings. Everything Else is the balance you actually use for day-to-day life and Fun.

2. Give every dollar a job

Chapter 7 divides money into Bills, Safety Vault, Life and Fun. The purpose is visibility: you can see what money is for rather than holding every category in your head.

3. Automate payday transfers

Standing orders can move money into Bills and Safety Vault automatically. The exact amounts should reflect your own income and obligations. The important system principle is to make the recurring decision once and let the transfer repeat.

4. Put boring bills on autopilot

Automation is especially useful for recurring bills and savings because missed dates can create fees and stress. Check the account setup occasionally so the automation itself keeps working.

5. Make one number easy to see

The book recommends a visible daily money number and a short weekly Money Date. The goal is not to watch every transaction constantly. It is to make the current state of your money easy to access.

6. Add friction to the spending you want to slow down

Remove saved cards from your biggest impulse-shopping sites, log out, turn off shopping notifications and use a 24-hour Wish List. Small barriers can create the time needed for a deliberate decision.

7. Use rules instead of repeated decisions

For bigger purchases, the book recommends setting thresholds in advance. A purchase above your chosen threshold can require sleeping on it and, if useful, showing it to another person. The rule is created before the shiny thing appears.

8. Keep Fun money

A system that makes every enjoyable purchase feel wrong can be difficult to live with. The book gives Fun money a defined place. This makes enjoyment part of the plan instead of an exception to it.

9. Schedule the maintenance

Use a 15-minute weekly Money Date, a monthly two-hour Money Sprint and a quarterly Money Checkup. The schedule reduces the need to remember when to review the system.

10. Build a recovery protocol

Systems will sometimes break. The book's Chapter 10 approach is zero-judgment recovery: identify what failed, repair the system and restart. Missing a day does not have to mean missing the month.

One-line version: You do not need to become a perfect budgeter. You need a money system that is easy enough to keep using.
